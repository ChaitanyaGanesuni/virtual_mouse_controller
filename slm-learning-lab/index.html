What it means for a model to "learn language"

real computation · n-gram model

This is a real (tiny) language model, trained in your browser by counting which word follows which. Drag training data and watch it get better: its predictions sharpen and its loss on unseen sentences falls. A Transformer learns the same thing, P(next token | previous tokens), with gradient descent in place of counting.

Cross-entropy L = −(1/N)·Σ log P(actual next word). Lower is better. Perplexity = eL, roughly "how many words the model is torn between".

What the model learned (raw counts after "")

P(w | prev) = count(prev, w) / count(prev). These counts are this model's parameters. An SLM keeps millions of learned weights in their place, which lets it generalise to contexts it never saw.

01
Scalars, vectors, matrices, tensors

        
Everything inside an SLM is a box of numbers. What changes is how many directions the box extends in. Think of it as one spreadsheet cell, then a row, then a whole sheet, then a workbook of sheets.

      

        

          

          

          

        

        

          
Shape

          

        

      

      

        
Formula
x ∈ ℝd₁ × d₂ × … × dₖ

        
What the symbols mean
  • ℝ: real numbers (decimals)
  • dᵢ: size along axis i
  • k: rank, the number of axes

        
Inside an SLMToken IDs [B, T] → embeddings [B, T, d] → attention scores [B, H, T, T] → logits [B, T, V]. Getting shapes right is most of the work in Stage 6.

      

    


    
    

      
02
Vectors & the dot product
drag the arrow tips
        
A vector is an arrow. The dot product answers one question: how much do two arrows point the same way? Same direction gives a big positive number, perpendicular gives zero, and opposite gives a negative number.

      

        

          
          

            Word arrows (illustrative)
            
            
            
          

        

        

          

          

        

      

      

        
Formula
a · b = Σᵢ aᵢ bᵢ = |a| |b| cos θ

        
What the symbols mean
  • aᵢ, bᵢ: the i-th numbers of each vector
  • |a|: length of arrow a
  • θ: the angle between the arrows

        
Inside an SLMAttention compares tokens with q · k: a big dot product means "this word is relevant to me". Cosine similarity, cos θ, measures how alike two word embeddings are. Real vectors have 64–4096 numbers, and it's the same sum with more terms.

A model's raw scores (logits) can be any numbers: negative, huge, whatever. Softmax turns them into percentages that add up to 100%. It keeps the ranking but stretches the gaps. Move the scores and watch all three stages update.

05
Loss & cross-entropy: scoring a guess
live
        
How do we tell the model how wrong it was? Look only at the probability it gave to the right answer. If it was high, the penalty is tiny. If it was low, the penalty is huge. That penalty is the loss, and training is the job of making it small.

      

        

          
          
          
        

        

          
Penalty for this guess

          
What the model predicted

          

        

      

      

        
Formula
L = −log pcorrect  ·  averaged over all tokens

        
What the symbols mean
  • pcorrect: probability given to the true next token
  • log is the natural log (ln)
  • L = 0 only if p = 1

        
Inside an SLMThe training loss is the mean cross-entropy over every token in a batch. Perplexity = eL (you met it in Stage 0). A random guess over V words gives L = ln V, e.g. ln 8000 ≈ 8.99 for an untrained model.

      

    


    
    

      
06
Derivatives & gradients: measuring steepness
drag the dot
        
A derivative is the steepness of a curve at one point: nudge x a tiny bit, and how much does f move? Make the step h smaller and the orange line turns into the green tangent. That limit is the derivative.

      

        

          

          
          
          

        

        

          

          

        

      

      

        
Formula
f′(x) = limh→0 (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h

        
What the symbols mean
  • h: a tiny nudge to x
  • f′(x): the slope at x
  • ∇L = [∂L/∂w₁, ∂L/∂w₂, …]: one slope per weight

        
Inside an SLMThe gradient of the loss has one slope per parameter. A 1M-parameter model computes a million slopes on every training step. Each one tells its weight which way is downhill.

      

    


    
    

      
07
Gradient descent: rolling downhill
real optimisation
        
Training is like walking downhill blindfolded: feel the slope under your feet, step downhill, repeat. The map shows the loss for every combination of two weights: dark means low loss, bright means high. Click anywhere on the map to drop the ball there.

      

        

          

          

            
          

        

        

          

            

            
            
          

          

          
Loss after each step

          

        

      

      

        
Formula
w ← w − η · ∇L(w)

        
What the symbols mean
  • w: the weights (here only 2)
  • η: learning rate, the step size
  • ∇L: the gradient, which points uphill

        
Inside an SLMThis is optimizer.step(), applied to every weight at once in a space with millions of dimensions. AdamW (Stage 8) adds momentum and a separate step size for each weight.

      

    


    
    

      
08
Chain rule & backpropagation: assigning blame
real gradients
        
The forward pass makes a prediction. The backward pass works out how much each weight is to blame for the error by multiplying local slopes along the path. That's the chain rule. Then each weight takes a small step to reduce its blame. Press the buttons in order.

      

        

        

        

          
          
          
          
          
        

      

      

        

          
          
          
          
          
        

        

          

          
Loss over updates

        

      

      

        
Formula
∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂a · ∂a/∂z · ∂z/∂w

        
What the symbols mean
  • ∂L/∂w: how much L changes if w nudges
  • Each factor is a local slope of one step
  • Multiply them along the path back to w

        
Inside an SLMloss.backward() runs this exact rule backwards through embeddings, attention, feed-forward layers and the LM head, for every weight. It's the same idea as here, only with millions of nodes.

How words become numbers: tokenization

      
A neural network only does arithmetic. This stage follows a sentence from the letters you type to the integer tensor the model receives, and you build a real BPE tokenizer one merge at a time along the way.

    

    

      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
    


    
    

      
01
The tokenizer pipeline, end to end
real tokenizer
        
Every SLM has a tokenizer in front of it: a translator that turns text into a list of integers, and back again. Type anything and press Play to see all eight steps. The rest of this stage looks at each step closely.

      

        
        

      

      

      

    


    
    

      
02
Text is already numbers: Unicode & UTF-8
your browser's real encoding
        
Your computer already stores every character as bytes (numbers from 0 to 255). English letters take 1 byte, accented letters 2, the rupee sign 3 and emoji 4. Some tokenizers start from these 256 bytes, so they never meet a character they can't handle.

      

        

          
          

        

        

          

          

        

      

      

        
Key idea
character → code point (U+…) → 1–4 bytes

        
What the terms mean
  • Code point: Unicode's number for a character
  • UTF-8: the rule that stores a code point as 1–4 bytes
  • Byte-level BPE: BPE that starts from the 256 bytes

        
Inside an SLMGPT-2-style tokenizers are byte-level, so emoji, Hindi or a typo never become <unk>. The catch: text with rare characters costs more tokens. A 4-byte emoji can take several.

      

    


    
    

      
03
Normalization: cleaning before cutting
live
        
The same word can be typed many ways: Bank, BANK, bank, or "café" stored as one character or as two. Normalization rewrites text into one standard form so these variants become the same token. Switch each rule on and off and watch the text change.

      

        

          
          

          

        

        

          
Seven variants of two words

          

        

      

      

        
Common rules
NFKC · lowercase · strip accents · collapse spaces · mask URLs/numbers

        
Trade-offs
  • Lowercasing merges "US" and "us"
  • Stripping accents merges "résumé" and "resume"
  • Masking numbers loses "₹500 vs ₹5000"

        
Inside an SLMModern SLM tokenizers normalize lightly (usually NFKC only) and keep case. The model learns that Bank and bank are related. For a banking SLM, never mask amounts or account formats: they are the data.

      

    


    
    

      
04
Character vs word vs subword (BPE)
three real tokenizers, trained on this lab's corpus
        
Where do we cut the text? Into characters (tiny vocabulary, very long sequences), whole words (short sequences, but every new word is unknown), or subwords (common words whole, rare words in pieces). Each coloured box below is one token.

      

        
        
      

      

      

      

        

        
All three were trained on the same ~50 sentences (Stage 0's corpus plus banking text) and the input is lowercased first. Try a typo like mortgaeg, or a new word like overdrafts. Then slide the BPE merges to 0 (BPE becomes characters) and to its maximum (BPE becomes almost whole words, because merges stop once no pair appears twice).

      

      

        
Vocabulary size V
char ≈ 40 · BPE = base + merges · word = every word seen

        
What to watch
  • Tokens: sequence length T
  • <unk>: information lost forever
  • V × d: embedding parameters

        
Inside an SLMNearly every modern SLM uses subwords (BPE, WordPiece or Unigram) with V = 8K–128K. Common words stay whole, rare ones split into known pieces, and nothing becomes <unk>.

      

    


    
    

      
05
Byte Pair Encoding, one merge at a time
real BPE training
        
BPE builds a vocabulary bottom-up: start from single characters, find the pair of neighbours that appears most often, glue it into a new token, repeat. The ordered list of merges is the trained tokenizer. ▁ marks the start of a word.

      

        
        Corpus

    Algorithm
    repeat: (a, b) = most frequent neighbour pair → replace every a b with ab → add ab to vocab
    
        
    What the parts mean
    • Pair count: Σ word frequency × occurrences
    • Merge rule: (a, b) → ab, applied in order
    • Vocab size = base symbols + number of merges
    
        
    Inside an SLMGPT-2 learned 50,000 merges this way on 40 GB of text. Encoding a new string replays the same merges in the same order, so the result is deterministic and never unknown.

    Stage 03 · unlocked
    
      
    Giving numbers meaning: embeddings
    
      
    A token ID like 46 is just a name tag. This stage turns each ID into a list of numbers whose geometry captures meaning. Similar words land close together, directions encode relationships, and a position signal tells the model where each word sits.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
    
    

    
    
    
      
    01
    Why can't the model just use the ID numbers?
    illustrative vectors
        
    IDs are arbitrary: "cat" = 12 and "dog" = 13 are neighbours only by accident. One-hot vectors (all zeros, a single 1) fix that, but they make every word equally unrelated to every other. Dense embeddings give each word a short list of real numbers in which similar words get similar numbers. Flip between the two and compare the similarity grids.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    
        
    Cosine similarity between every pair
    
      
    
      
    
        
    One-hot
    onehot(i) ∈ {0,1}V, cos = 0 for any two different words
    
        
    Dense embedding
    • A row of real numbers, length d ≪ V
    • V = 32,000 but d = 256: about 125× shorter
    • Similar meaning → similar direction
    
        
    Inside an SLMThe very first layer of every SLM is an embedding table. All later layers only ever see these vectors, never the IDs.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    02
    The embedding matrix: a lookup table of vectors
    illustrative values · real mechanics
        
    The embedding matrix E has one row per vocabulary word and d columns. "Embedding a token" just means copying the row at its ID. Press Play to watch each token fetch its row, then change d to make every row wider or narrower.
    
      
    
        
        
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    E: shape 
    
        
    
          
    Output: one row per token
    
          
    Same thing as a matrix multiplication
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    xt = E[idt] = onehot(idt) · E
    
        
    Shapes
    • E: [V, d], learnable
    • ids: [T] → output: [T, d]
    • Batch: [B, T] → [B, T, d]
    
        
    Inside an SLMIn PyTorch this is nn.Embedding(V, d). Its V × d numbers start random and are trained by backpropagation like every other weight (Stage 1, panel 08).

    04
    Cosine similarity: measuring how alike two words are
    live calculation
        
    Cosine similarity compares directions and ignores lengths: +1 is the same direction, 0 is unrelated, −1 is opposite. It's the dot product from Stage 1, divided by both lengths. Try "Stretch A ×2": the dot product doubles but the cosine doesn't move.
    
      
    
        
        
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    
        
    
          
    −1 opposite0 unrelated+1 same
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| |b|)
    
        
    What the symbols mean
    • a · b: Σ aᵢbᵢ (Stage 1)
    • |a|: √(Σ aᵢ²), the length
    • Range: −1 … +1
    
        
    Inside an SLMSemantic search, retrieval (RAG) and duplicate detection rank texts by cosine similarity of their embeddings. Inside attention the raw dot product is used, so length matters there.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    05
    Positional encoding: a fingerprint for every position
    exact sinusoidal formula
        
    Attention looks at all tokens at once, so on its own it doesn't know their order. We add a position vector to each token. The classic recipe uses waves: low dimensions wiggle fast, high dimensions slowly, like the second, minute and hour hands of a clock. Each position gets a unique fingerprint. Hover over the heatmap.
    
      
    
        
        
      
    
      
    
        
    PE[position, dimension]: 64 positions × d
    
          
          
    
        
    
          
    Four dimensions as waves across positions
    
          
    Similarity PE(p) · PE(q): nearby positions look alike
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/d) · PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/d)
    
        
    What the symbols mean
    • pos: token position 0, 1, 2…
    • i: dimension pair index
    • 100002i/d: sets each wave's wavelength
    
        
    Inside an SLMGPT-2 uses learned position embeddings, an [C, d] table trained like E. Most modern SLMs use RoPE, which rotates Q and K by position-dependent angles from these same sinusoids. Both give every position a unique signal.

    • uw, uc: vectors of a word and its context word
    • n: randomly drawn "negative" words
    • σ: sigmoid, which turns a dot product into a probability
    
        
    Inside an SLMAn SLM doesn't train embeddings separately. Its embedding rows get gradients from the next-token loss along with everything else. The effect is the same: tokens used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors.

    Neural networks: neurons, layers and learning
    
      
    Every SLM is built from one tiny part, repeated millions of times: a neuron that adds up weighted inputs and bends the result. This stage starts with one neuron, finds out what it can't do, and then builds a network that can. You can add neurons, drag weights and watch it learn.
    
    
    
    
    
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
      
    
    

    
    
    
      
    01
    The perceptron: one neuron drawing one line
    real learning rule
        
    A perceptron weighs its inputs, adds a bias, and answers yes (1) or no (0). Geometrically, it draws one straight line and says yes on one side. Move the sliders, or press Learn and watch it fix its own mistakes. Then try XOR.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
          
        
    
        
    
          
    
            
            
            
          
    
          
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    ŷ = step(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b) · learning rule: w ← w + η (y − ŷ) x
    
        
    What the symbols mean
    • x₁, x₂: inputs
    • w, b: weights and bias, the things it learns
    • ŷ: prediction, y: correct answer
    
        
    Inside an SLMEvery neuron in an SLM is this same weighted sum. The difference is a smooth activation instead of a hard step, so gradients can flow (Stage 1, panel 08), and millions of neurons working together instead of one.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    02
    Activation functions: the bend that makes networks powerful
    exact functions
        
    After the weighted sum, every neuron passes its value z through an activation function. Without that bend, stacking layers is pointless, because many straight lines combined are still one straight line. Pick a function, drag z, and compare its slope, which is what backpropagation needs.
    
      
    
        
    
          
    
          
          
          
        
    
        
    
          
    
          
    Why bend at all? A 1 → 3 → 1 network, same weights
    
            
    
            
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formulas
    ReLU(z) = max(0, z) · σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z) · tanh(z) · GELU(z) ≈ z·σ(1.702 z)
    
        
    What to compare
    • Output range: ReLU 0…∞, σ 0…1, tanh −1…1
    • Slope: σ and tanh flatten at the ends (vanishing gradients)
    • ReLU's slope is exactly 0 for z < 0 ("dead" neurons)
    
        
    Inside an SLMThe feed-forward layers of GPT-2 and most SLMs use GELU (or the closely related SwiGLU), a smooth ReLU that keeps a small gradient for negative values. Sigmoid survives inside gates. Tanh mostly appears in older RNNs.

    From this network to an SLM's feed-forward block
    illustrative values · exact shapes and counts
        
    Inside every Transformer block there is a small neural network just like the one you built: widen each token's vector 4×, apply GELU, then shrink it back. It runs on every token separately.     Formula
    FFN(x) = W₂ · GELU(W₁ x + b₁) + b₂
    
        
    Shapes

    • W₁: [d, 4d] → hidden [T, 4d]
    • W₂: [4d, d] → output [T, d]
    
        
    Inside an SLMThe FFN holds about two-thirds of each block's parameters, roughly 8d² of the block's 12d². Research suggests much of a model's factual knowledge is stored in these weights. Attention (Stage 5) moves information between tokens; the FFN processes each token on its own.

    The Transformer: attention is how words talk to each other

    01
    Why Transformers replaced RNNs and LSTMs
    illustrative timing & memory
        
    An RNN reads one word at a time and squeezes everything so far into a single memory vector, like passing a whispered message down a line. Early words fade, and nothing can run in parallel. A Transformer lets every word look at every other word directly, all at once. Press Race.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    RNN / LSTM: one step per word
    
        
    Transformer: all words in one step
    
      
    
      
    RNN / LSTMTransformer
    steps to read T tokensT (sequential)1 (parallel)
    path from word 1 to word TT − 1 hops (fades)1 hop (direct)
    uses GPU/CPU cores wellpoorlyvery well
    cost with long textgrows like Tgrows like T² (attention)
    
      
    
        
    RNN update
    hₜ = tanh(W hₜ₋₁ + U xₜ)
    
        
    The two problems
    • Sequential: step t must wait for step t − 1
    • Fading memory: gradients shrink over many steps (vanishing gradients)
    • LSTMs add gates that help, but don't remove the bottleneck
    
        
    Inside an SLMThe 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" dropped recurrence entirely. Training on whole sequences in parallel is what made today's language models practical to train.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    02
    Encoder, decoder, and decoder-only
    attention masks
        
    The difference between Transformer families is who is allowed to look at whom. Each grid shows which words (columns) each word (rows) can attend to. A bright cell means allowed.
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
        
    The three families
    • Encoder-only (BERT): sees both sides, good at understanding
    • Encoder-decoder (T5): reads input, writes output
    • Decoder-only (GPT, Llama, Phi): sees only the past, generates text
    
        
    Causal rule
    token i may attend to token j only if j ≤ i
    
        
    Inside an SLMAlmost every SLM is decoder-only. One simple objective (predict the next token) and one stack of identical blocks. That's what we build in Stage 6.

    04
    Self-attention, one step at a time
    illustrative weights · every number really computed
        
    Each word asks a question (Query), advertises what it contains (Key), and offers information (Value). Matching queries to keys decides how much each word listens to every earlier word. Step through all 8 stages. Click a word to follow its row, and click a score cell to see how it was computed.
    
      
    
        
        
        
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    Who is  listening to?
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dk + M ) · V
    
        
    What the symbols mean
    • Q = XWQ: what each word is looking for
    • K = XWK: what each word offers to be matched on
    • V = XWV: the information actually passed along
    • M: mask, −∞ above the diagonal
    
        
    Inside an SLMHere d = 4 and the weights are hand-picked so similar words attract. A trained SLM learns WQ, WK, WV (each [d, d]) by backprop, and runs this in every block for every head.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    05
    Multi-head attention: several ways of listening at once
    illustrative heads
        
    One attention pattern can't capture everything. Multi-head attention runs several smaller attentions side by side. Each head can specialise: one tracks the previous word, one finds related words, one keeps an eye on the start. Their outputs are glued together and mixed by WO.
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formula
    MHA(X) = Concat(head₁, …, headH) · WO, headh = Attention(XWQh, XWKh, XWVh)
    
        
    Shapes
    • each head works in dk = d / H dims
    • concat: [T, H·dk] = [T, d]
    • WO: [d, d]. Total params 4d², whatever H is
    
        
    Inside an SLMResearchers really do find heads like these in trained models: "previous-token" heads, heads that copy repeated patterns (induction heads), and heads that attend to the first token as a resting place.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    06
    Residual connections & layer normalisation
    exact LayerNorm · illustrative gradients
        
    Two small tricks make deep stacks trainable. A residual connection adds each block's input back to its output (x + f(x)), creating a highway that gradients can travel straight down. LayerNorm rescales each token's vector to a standard size so numbers never explode or vanish.
    
      
    
        
    LayerNorm on one token's vector (d = 8)
    
          
    
          
    
        
    Gradient reaching each block, from the loss at the top
    
          
          
    
      
    
      
    LN(x) = γ · (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε) + β  ·  block: x ← x + MHA(LN(x)); x ← x + FFN(LN(x))
    
    
    

    
    
    
      
    07
    The context window: how much the model can see
    exact memory arithmetic
        
    A model can only attend to its last C tokens, its context window. Anything older is simply gone. And because every token attends to every other, the attention grid costs C × C: double the window and that grid is 4× bigger.
    
      
    
        
    
        
    
          
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Formulas
    attention scores per layer = B · H · C² · bytes  ·  KV cache = 2 · N · C · d · bytes
    
        
    Assumed model
    • d = 768, N = 12, H = 12 (GPT-2 small size)
    • FP16 (2 bytes), batch B = 1
    
        
    Inside an SLMSmall models typically use C = 512–4096. Learned position embeddings simply have no row beyond C. Tricks like sliding windows, FlashAttention and RoPE scaling stretch it.
